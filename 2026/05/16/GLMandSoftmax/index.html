GLMs and Softmax Regression

This article is a comprehensive overview of Generalized Linear Models (GLMs) and their applications in binary and multiclass classification, specifically focusing on Logistic Regression and Softmax Regression. The most ideas and mathematical derivations are based on the lecture notes from Stanford’s CS229 and CS231n courses, with additional insights and explanations which comes from the talk between I and Gemini to enhance understanding.

1. Introduction to Generalized Linear Models (GLMs)

In machine learning, we often encounter different types of response variables. For instance, in linear regression, we predict a continuous value y∈Ry \in \mathbb{R}y∈R given input features xxx. In binary classification, we predict a discrete label y∈{0,1}y \in \{0, 1\}y∈{0,1}. While these two problems seem different, they are actually special cases of a broader, unified statistical framework known as Generalized Linear Models (GLMs).

To deeply understand GLMs, we must first understand the foundation upon which they are built: the Exponential Family of distributions.

1.1 The Exponential Family

A class of distributions belongs to the exponential family if its probability density function (or probability mass function for discrete variables) can be expressed in the following canonical form:

p(y;η)=b(y)exp⁡(ηTT(y)−a(η))p(y; \eta) = b(y) \exp(\eta^T T(y) - a(\eta)) p(y;η)=b(y)exp(ηTT(y)−a(η))

Where:

  • η\etaη (eta) is called the natural parameter (or canonical parameter) of the distribution.
  • T(y)T(y)T(y) is the sufficient statistic. In many common distributions, T(y)=yT(y) = yT(y)=y.
  • a(η)a(\eta)a(η) is the log partition function. The term exp⁡(−a(η))\exp(-a(\eta))exp(−a(η)) acts as a normalization constant ensuring that the distribution integrates or sums to 1 over all possible values of yyy.
  • b(y)b(y)b(y) is a base measure, which is a function of yyy alone.

By choosing different functions for TTT, aaa, and bbb, we can define different families of distributions parameterized by η\etaη. Many common distributions, including Gaussian, Bernoulli, Multinomial, Poisson, Gamma, and Exponential distributions, belong to the exponential family.

The Bernoulli distribution defines a probability distribution over a binary random variable y∈{0,1}y \in \{0, 1\}y∈{0,1} with mean ϕ\phiϕ. Let’s demonstrate that it belongs to the exponential family.

The probability mass function is:

1.4 The Three Assumptions of GLMs

To construct a Generalized Linear Model for predicting a variable yyy given xxx, we make three fundamental design choices (assumptions):

  1. Distribution Assumption: Given xxx and parameters θ\thetaθ, the conditional distribution of yyy belongs to the Exponential Family with natural parameter η\etaη. i.e., y∣x;θ∼ExponentialFamily(η)y|x;\theta \sim \text{ExponentialFamily}(\eta)y∣x;θ∼ExponentialFamily(η).
  2. Expectation Assumption: Given xxx, our goal is to predict the expected value of T(y)T(y)T(y). Since T(y)=yT(y) = yT(y)=y in most cases, our hypothesis function hθ(x)h_\theta(x)hθ​(x) must satisfy hθ(x)=E[y∣x]h_\theta(x) = E[y|x]hθ​(x)=E[y∣x].
  3. Linearity Assumption: The natural parameter η\etaη and the inputs xxx are linearly related: η=θTx\eta = \theta^T xη=θTx.

With these three assumptions, we can derive algorithms for continuous regression, binary classification, and multiclass classification purely mathematically.

2. Binary Classification & Logistic Regression

In binary classification problems, the target yyy can take on only two values, typically y∈{0,1}y \in \{0, 1\}y∈{0,1} or y∈{−1,+1}y \in \{-1, +1\}y∈{−1,+1}. If we use ordinary linear regression (hθ(x)=θTxh_\theta(x) = \theta^T xhθ​(x)=θTx), the predictions can easily exceed 1 or fall below 0, which does not make sense for probabilities. To fix this, we apply Logistic Regression.

2.1 Logistic Regression as a GLM

Let’s derive Logistic Regression using the GLM recipe. Since y∈{0,1}y \in \{0, 1\}y∈{0,1}, we choose the Bernoulli distribution to model the conditional distribution of yyy given xxx.

  1. By GLM Assumption 1, y∣x;θ∼Bernoulli(ϕ)y|x;\theta \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\phi)y∣x;θ∼Bernoulli(ϕ).
  2. From our exponential family formulation of Bernoulli, we know ϕ=11+e−η\phi = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\eta}}ϕ=1+e−η1​.
  3. By GLM Assumption 2, our hypothesis outputs the expected value: hθ(x)=E[y∣x;θ]=p(y=1∣x;θ)=ϕh_\theta(x) = E[y|x;\theta] = p(y=1|x;\theta) = \phihθ​(x)=E[y∣x;θ]=p(y=1∣x;θ)=ϕ.
  4. By GLM Assumption 3, η=θTx\eta = \theta^T xη=θTx.

Putting it all together:

hθ(x)=11+exp⁡(−θTx)=g(θTx)h_\theta(x) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-\theta^T x)} = g(\theta^T x) hθ​(x)=1+exp(−θTx)1​=g(θTx)

Where g(z)=11+e−zg(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}g(z)=1+e−z1​ is the sigmoid function. This perfectly demonstrates that the logistic function is not arbitrarily chosen; it arises naturally from the assumption that the data follows a Bernoulli distribution and the rules of GLMs.

3. Margin-Based Loss Functions in Binary Classification

For a deeper understanding of classification, let us shift the labels from {0,1}\{0, 1\}{0,1} to {−1,+1}\{-1, +1\}{−1,+1}. In this regime, the hypothesis makes predictions based on the sign of θTx\theta^T xθTx.

predict=sign(θTx)\text{predict} = \text{sign}(\theta^T x) predict=sign(θTx)

3.1 The Concept of Margin

We correctly classify an example if yyy and θTx\theta^T xθTx have the same sign. Thus, classification is correct if:

yθTx>0y \theta^T x > 0 yθTx>0

The product z=yθTxz = y \theta^T xz=yθTx is a critically important quantity known as the margin.

  • If z>0z > 0z>0, the classification is correct. A large positive zzz means high confidence in the correct label.
  • If z<0z < 0z<0, the classification is incorrect. A large negative zzz means high confidence in the wrong label.

Our goal is to define a loss function φ(z)\varphi(z)φ(z) that penalizes negative margins heavily and assigns low loss to positive margins. The empirical risk we want to minimize is:

J(θ)=1m∑i=1mφ(y(i)θTx(i))J(\theta) = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \varphi(y^{(i)} \theta^T x^{(i)}) J(θ)=m1​i=1∑m​φ(y(i)θTx(i))

3.2 Common Loss Functions

Here are four common loss functions φ(z)\varphi(z)φ(z) mapped against the margin zzz:

  1. Zero-One Loss (φzo\varphi_{zo}φzo​):

    φzo(z)={1if z≤00if z>0\varphi_{zo}(z) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } z \le 0 \\ 0 & \text{if } z > 0 \end{cases} φzo​(z)={10​if z≤0if z>0​

    This strictly counts the number of misclassifications. However, it is discontinuous, non-convex, and NP-hard to minimize. Therefore, we use surrogate loss functions.

  2. Hinge Loss (φhinge\varphi_{hinge}φhinge​):

    φhinge(z)=max⁡(0,1−z)\varphi_{hinge}(z) = \max(0, 1 - z) φhinge​(z)=max(0,1−z)

    This loss forms the basis of Support Vector Machines (SVMs). It penalizes predictions that are not only incorrect but also those that are correct but not confident enough (margin < 1).

  3. Logistic Loss (φlogistic\varphi_{logistic}φlogistic​):

    φlogistic(z)=log⁡(1+e−z)\varphi_{logistic}(z) = \log(1 + e^{-z}) φlogistic​(z)=log(1+e−z)

    This is the loss optimized in Logistic Regression. It is a smooth, convex curve that goes to 000 as z→∞z \rightarrow \inftyz→∞ and goes to infinity as z→−∞z \rightarrow -\inftyz→−∞.

  4. Exponential Loss (φexp\varphi_{exp}φexp​):

    φexp(z)=e−z\varphi_{exp}(z) = e^{-z} φexp​(z)=e−z

    This gives rise to the classical AdaBoost algorithm. It penalizes negative margins exponentially, making it very sensitive to outliers.

4. Multiclass Classification: Softmax Regression

What happens when we want to classify emails not just as “Spam” or “Not Spam”, but into kkk distinct categories: y∈{1,2,…,k}y \in \{1, 2, \dots, k\}y∈{1,2,…,k}? We must generalize Logistic Regression into Softmax Regression.

4.1 Multinomial Distribution as an Exponential Family

To derive Softmax Regression via GLMs, we model the response variable yyy using a Multinomial Distribution. We have kkk possible outcomes, parameterized by probabilities ϕ1,ϕ2,…,ϕk\phi_1, \phi_2, \dots, \phi_kϕ1​,ϕ2​,…,ϕk​. Since the probabilities must sum to 1 (∑i=1kϕi=1\sum_{i=1}^k \phi_i = 1∑i=1k​ϕi​=1), there are only k−1k-1k−1 independent parameters. We define ϕk=1−∑i=1k−1ϕi\phi_k = 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} \phi_iϕk​=1−∑i=1k−1​ϕi​.

We define the sufficient statistic T(y)∈Rk−1T(y) \in \mathbb{R}^{k-1}T(y)∈Rk−1 as a vector where the iii-th element is an indicator function 1{y=i}1\{y = i\}1{y=i}.

Expressing the Multinomial probability distribution:

p(y;ϕ)=ϕ11{y=1}ϕ21{y=2}⋯ϕk1{y=k}=ϕ1(T(y))1ϕ2(T(y))2⋯ϕk1−∑i=1k−1(T(y))i=exp⁡(∑i=1k−1(T(y))ilog⁡(ϕi)+(1−∑i=1k−1(T(y))i)log⁡(ϕk))=exp⁡(∑i=1k−1(T(y))ilog⁡(ϕiϕk)+log⁡(ϕk))\begin{aligned} p(y; \phi) &= \phi_1^{1\{y=1\}} \phi_2^{1\{y=2\}} \cdots \phi_k^{1\{y=k\}} \\ &= \phi_1^{(T(y))_1} \phi_2^{(T(y))_2} \cdots \phi_k^{1 - \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} (T(y))_i} \\ &= \exp \left( \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} (T(y))_i \log(\phi_i) + \left(1 - \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} (T(y))_i\right) \log(\phi_k) \right) \\ &= \exp \left( \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} (T(y))_i \log\left(\frac{\phi_i}{\phi_k}\right) + \log(\phi_k) \right) \end{aligned} p(y;ϕ)​=ϕ11{y=1}​ϕ21{y=2}​⋯ϕk1{y=k}​=ϕ1(T(y))1​​ϕ2(T(y))2​​⋯ϕk1−∑i=1k−1​(T(y))i​​=exp(i=1∑k−1​(T(y))i​log(ϕi​)+(1−i=1∑k−1​(T(y))i​)log(ϕk​))=exp(i=1∑k−1​(T(y))i​log(ϕk​ϕi​​)+log(ϕk​))​

By matching terms with the exponential family equation, the natural parameter vector η∈Rk−1\eta \in \mathbb{R}^{k-1}η∈Rk−1 is defined such that its iii-th element is:

ηi=log⁡(ϕiϕk)\eta_i = \log\left(\frac{\phi_i}{\phi_k}\right) ηi​=log(ϕk​ϕi​​)

We can invert the link function to solve for ϕi\phi_iϕi​ in terms of ηi\eta_iηi​:

By GLM Assumption 3, we define linear predictors for each class: ηi=θiTx\eta_i = \theta_i^T xηi​=θiT​x. Thus, the conditional probability of predicting class iii given input xxx is:

4.3 Cross-Entropy Loss Function

To train the Softmax Regression model, we maximize the log-likelihood of the parameters:

ℓ(θ)=∑i=1mlog⁡p(y(i)∣x(i);θ)=∑i=1mlog⁡∏l=1k(exp⁡(θlTx(i))∑j=1kexp⁡(θjTx(i)))1{y(i)=l}\ell(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^m \log p(y^{(i)}|x^{(i)}; \theta) = \sum_{i=1}^m \log \prod_{l=1}^k \left( \frac{\exp(\theta_l^T x^{(i)})}{\sum_{j=1}^k \exp(\theta_j^T x^{(i)})} \right)^{1\{y^{(i)}=l\}} ℓ(θ)=i=1∑m​logp(y(i)∣x(i);θ)=i=1∑m​logl=1∏k​(∑j=1k​exp(θjT​x(i))exp(θlT​x(i))​)1{y(i)=l}

Li=−log⁡(exp⁡(θyiTxi)∑j=1kexp⁡(θjTxi))L_i = -\log \left( \frac{\exp(\theta_{y_i}^T x_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^k \exp(\theta_j^T x_i)} \right) Li​=−log(∑j=1k​exp(θjT​xi​)exp(θyi​T​xi​)​)

L=1m∑i=1mLiL = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m L_i L=m1​i=1∑m​Li​

The softmax function takes a vector of arbitrary raw class scores (unnormalized log probabilities) and squashes them into a vector of values between 0 and 1 that sum to 1. The Cross-Entropy loss then penalizes the model based on how far the computed probability of the correct class is from 1.

5. Softmax Classifier vs. Multiclass SVM (CS231n Perspective)

When classifying images (e.g., in deep learning contexts like CS231n), two classifiers are predominantly used at the end of neural networks: the Softmax Classifier and the Multiclass Support Vector Machine (SVM).

5.1 Multiclass Support Vector Machine (SVM) Loss

The Multiclass SVM aims to ensure that the score of the correct class is higher than the scores of all incorrect classes by a fixed margin Δ\DeltaΔ (usually set to 1.0).
Given an input xix_ixi​ and correct label yiy_iyi​, let sj=WjTxis_j = W_j^T x_isj​=WjT​xi​ be the score of the jjj-th class. The SVM loss for the iii-th example is defined as the sum of hinge losses over all incorrect classes:

Li=∑j≠yimax⁡(0,sj−syi+Δ)L_i = \sum_{j \neq y_i} \max(0, s_j - s_{y_i} + \Delta) Li​=j=yi​∑​max(0,sj​−syi​​+Δ)

L=1N∑i=1NLi+λR(W)L = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N L_i + \lambda R(W) L=N1​i=1∑N​Li​+λR(W)

5.2 Differences in Interpretation and Optimization

While both classifiers compute a raw score vector s=Wxs = W xs=Wx, they interpret and penalize these scores very differently.

  1. Interpretation of Scores:

    • SVM: Interprets raw outputs purely as class scores. It doesn’t care about their exact values, only their relative differences. As long as the correct class score beats the incorrect ones by the margin Δ\DeltaΔ, the SVM loss is zero.
    • Softmax: Interprets raw scores as unnormalized log probabilities. It passes them through the softmax function to yield normalized probabilities. It strictly pushes the probability of the correct class toward 1 and incorrect classes toward 0.

  2. “Local” vs “Global” Objective:

    • The SVM is a “local” objective. If the scores for three classes are [10, -2, 3] and the correct class is the first one, the SVM margin is satisfied (10>3+110 > 3 + 110>3+1). The loss is exactly 0. If the scores changed to [10, 9, 9], the loss increases. But changing from [10, -100, -100] to [10, -2, 3] doesn’t change the loss (it stays 0).
    • The Softmax classifier is a “global” objective that is never fully satisfied. For [10, -2, 3], the softmax probability for class 1 is p1≈0.999p_1 \approx 0.999p1​≈0.999, so the cross-entropy loss is very small but not identically 0. If the scores were [10, -100, -100], p1p_1p1​ gets even closer to 1, and the loss decreases further. Softmax will infinitely attempt to drive the correct class score to +∞+\infty+∞ and incorrect to −∞-\infty−∞.

  3. Probability Calibration:

    • Softmax explicitly provides normalized confidence “probabilities”. However, these probabilities are sensitive to the regularization strength λ\lambdaλ. A high λ\lambdaλ penalizes large weights, causing the scores to shrink closer to zero, making the resulting probabilities more diffuse (closer to uniform).
